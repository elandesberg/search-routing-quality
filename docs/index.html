Problem framing

Compared to what?

Defining quality for AI-enhanced search — and the case for starting to learn our routing now.

author, team, date

Our search product has two paths. A shopper's query is either served by a traditional retrieval experience — fast, cheap, familiar — or routed to a generative path that returns enhanced results alongside a natural-language summary, at meaningfully higher latency and cost. Which path a query takes is decided by an allowlist, constructed heuristically and deliberately kept narrow.

That design has served us well as a way to ship safely. It has not given us a way to answer the two questions that now matter most: is the enhanced experience good, and are we routing to it correctly? This document argues that we cannot answer the second question with the data we currently collect, that the first question is meaningless until we say what we are comparing against, and that we should start buying routing data now — not instead of improving the experience, but because without it we cannot honestly measure whether the experience is improving.

The argument in briefFive claims

  1. Quality is not a property of the enhanced experience in isolation. It is a comparison, and the thing being compared against has to be named explicitly every time a quality claim is made.
  2. Because we are ultimately judged by A/B tests, quality should be defined as what an A/B test measures: the difference between what a shopper did when routed to the enhanced experience and what that same shopper would have done had they not been — net of the cost and latency the enhanced path imposes.
  3. Our current data cannot support that definition. Comparing allowlisted queries served enhanced results against other queries served traditional results confounds the effect of the experience with the selection of the allowlist. And for queries off the allowlist the problem is worse than bias: we have no relevant observations at all, so no amount of statistical adjustment recovers the answer.
  4. There are exactly two ways to improve the product — change which queries get routed, or change what the enhanced experience does when they arrive. These interact in both directions, and the interaction is the reason to sequence them deliberately rather than pursue them independently.
  5. The two axes can and should move in parallel — but exploration cannot wait. The routing gap is large, cheap to close, and the only improvement that can raise outcomes while lowering cost. More importantly, the exploration data it requires is the same data that makes experience improvements measurable outside today's narrow allowlist. Deferring it taxes both axes at once.

Part oneA feature is only as good as its relevant alternative

There is no absolute standard against which a generative overview is good or bad. An overview can be accurate, fluent, well-cited, and beautifully rendered, and still be worthless — if the traditional retrieval path would have put the same shopper in front of the same product just as reliably, at a fraction of the cost and a fraction of the latency. Conversely, a mediocre overview that rescues an ambiguous query the retrieval path fumbles badly is genuinely valuable, however unimpressive it looks in isolation.

This is not a pedantic point about measurement hygiene. It determines what we build. A team that evaluates the enhanced experience on its own terms will invest in making overviews more impressive. A team that evaluates it against the relevant alternative will invest in the queries where the alternative fails. Those are different roadmaps.

Three baselines, three different questions

"The relevant alternative" is not one thing. At least three baselines are live for us, and each answers a different question:

BaselineThe question it answersWho needs the answer
Our own traditional path, with enhanced routing switched off Does our AI enhancement add value over the search we would otherwise have shipped? Product and roadmap decisions — where to invest, what to route
A competing search vendor Would a retailer be better off with them than with us? Competitive positioning and displacement
The retailer's incumbent search Should this retailer buy anything at all? The actual purchase decision for a new customer

The team is currently focused on the first, and that is the right place to start: it is the baseline we control, the one we can randomize against, and the one that governs internal decisions about routing and investment. But it is worth being explicit that a result which looks flat against our own traditional path does not imply a flat result commercially. The incumbent search a prospective retailer is running today is frequently the weakest of the three baselines, and the enhancement may clear it comfortably while barely clearing our own. Conflating these produces confused conversations in which two people are both right about different questions.

Working discipline

Every quality claim names its baseline. "The enhanced experience improves engagement" is not a claim; "the enhanced experience improves engagement relative to our traditional path, on these queries, for these shoppers" is. Claims without a stated baseline should be treated as incomplete rather than debated.

The A/B test is not just how we are graded — it defines the quantity

We are evaluated by A/B tests, and this is more consequential than it appears. It means the quantity we care about is already specified for us: an A/B test measures the difference in outcomes between shoppers who received a treatment and otherwise-identical shoppers who did not. Any definition of quality that cannot be expressed as that kind of contrast is a definition we cannot be held to, and — more importantly — cannot act on. If our internal notion of "good" is not the thing the A/B test will register, we will be repeatedly surprised by our own launches. The definition in Part Two is built to add up to exactly what the test measures.

Part twoA definition of quality

The definition has two levels, and separating them is what makes the rest of the argument tractable. A small amount of notation makes both levels precise and is reused throughout; nothing beyond it will be needed.

Per query: incremental effect

For a given query in a given context — the shopper, the session, the catalog, the device — the enhanced experience performs well when it produces better outcomes for that query than the traditional path would have produced for that same query and that same shopper, net of the latency and cost it imposes.

Setup

Let x be a query together with its context — shopper, session, catalog, device. For each x there are two outcomes we could observe: Y(1) if it is served enhanced, Y(0) if it is served traditional — measured at the session or visitor level (see the metric discussion below). Any given eligible exposure shows us one; the other is the counterfactual. Per-query quality is the expected difference between them, net of serving cost:

τ(x) = E[ Y(1) − Y(0) | x ] − c(x)

where c(x) is the incremental cost of serving x on the enhanced path, priced in the same units as Y. Latency does not appear in c(x): it enters through Y(1) itself, because Y(1) is the outcome under the enhanced experience as actually delivered, delay included.

This query-level object is for learning, not the audited launch number. The primary product estimand remains the visitor-level intention-to-treat contrast between a routing policy and the status quo. For query learning, the clean default is each randomized visitor's first eligible query: later queries may have been caused or prevented by an earlier treatment, so conditioning on their occurrence can break the original randomization. All-query estimates can still be useful as sensitivity analyses, but they are not independent replications of the first-query contrast.

Three things follow from stating it this way. First, quality is conditional: τ varies across queries, and no single aggregate summarizes it without reference to which queries it is aggregated over. Second, it can be negative — there are certainly regions of x where the enhanced path makes things worse, and a definition that cannot express this is not useful for routing. Third, latency belongs inside the effect, not beside it as a budget line. Slower results depress engagement independently of how good they are, so a summary that is more useful but arrives noticeably later can have τ(x) < 0. Treating latency only as a cost constraint hides this; folding it into Y(1) surfaces it.

Across the product: captured value

The product as a whole performs well when the routing policy sends traffic to the enhanced path wherever the incremental effect is positive and large enough to justify the cost, and withholds it everywhere else — within whatever cost and latency budget we are willing to spend.

Policies and their value

A routing policy π assigns each query a probability of going to the enhanced path; today's allowlist is the policy that assigns 1 on the list and 0 off it. Relative to never routing, the value of a policy is

V(π) = E[ π(x) τ(x) ]the per-query effect, summed over the queries the policy routes, weighted by traffic

The product-level object to manage is the gap between V under today's policy and V under the best policy we could feasibly run within the cost and latency budget. That gap — not any single metric — is the value currently left on the table. The equation is the routing abstraction for a fixed stream of eligible queries. When routing changes which queries a visitor issues later, the visitor-level randomized contrast is authoritative; the per-query sum is an approximation used to learn a policy, not a substitute for that readout.

This decomposition earns its keep by separating two failures that are constantly conflated in discussion: the enhanced experience is weak on this query and we routed this query to the enhanced experience when it was never going to help. These have completely different fixes, and a single aggregate quality number cannot distinguish them.

What to measure, and the trap in the obvious answer

Click-through rate is the natural first instinct and a poor primary metric for this product specifically. If a natural-language summary actually answers the shopper's question, the shopper may click less while being better served — the well-documented "good abandonment" problem in search. A genuinely better experience can move CTR in the wrong direction. CTR is a useful diagnostic and a dangerous north star.

Relatedly, metrics have to be measured at the level where substitution can happen. A shopper who gets a good overview may issue fewer queries in the session. At the query level that looks like reduced engagement; at the session level it looks like a shopper who found what they wanted faster. Only the second reading is correct, and only session- or visitor-level measurement can see it.

TierRoleCandidates

Before analysis, the north star needs a metric contract: randomization and analysis unit, denominator, attribution window, delayed-outcome cutoff, and censoring rules. The binomial query model used later applies only when that contract yields one binary outcome contribution per randomized unit. Revenue, counts, repeated sessions, and other non-binary outcomes need a likelihood and uncertainty calculation that match their distribution.

Part threeWhy our current comparison cannot support that definition

The comparison most readily available to us is some version of: take the allowlisted queries that received enhanced results, take the queries that did not, and compare their outcomes. That number is the true effect on the allowlisted queries plus whatever baseline difference exists between the traffic on and off the allowlist — and the second piece is zero only if the allowlist were a random draw from traffic, which it deliberately is not. So the comparison mixes the thing we want with a term of unknown size; and even the first piece only describes the allowlist, saying nothing about τ(x) anywhere else.

This failure has three distinct sources. They are worth separating, because they call for different remedies and only one of them is fatal.

1. Composition

Allowlisted and non-allowlisted queries are not the same kind of query, and are not issued by the same kind of shopper. They differ in intent, specificity, commercial value, position in the head or tail of the distribution, and where the shopper is in their journey. Consider a long, comparison-shaped natural-language query about which product suits a particular need, against an exact product-and-model lookup. The first is typically issued by someone early in a research process; the second by someone close to buying. Their click and conversion rates differ enormously regardless of what we show either of them. A comparison across these two groups measures the difference between a researcher and a buyer, not the difference between two search experiences.

This is the most familiar problem and the most tractable — in principle it can be partially addressed by adjusting for observable query and shopper characteristics, to the extent we have measured the right ones.

2. Selection on the outcome

The allowlist was constructed heuristically, with the explicit intent of picking queries where the enhancement was expected to help. This means the treated set is selected on precisely the quantity we are trying to measure. Whatever judgment went into building the list — pattern recognition, intuition about which queries the retrieval path handles badly, caution about queries where a wrong summary would be embarrassing — is largely unrecorded, and therefore cannot be adjusted for. Adjustment can only correct for things we wrote down.

Neither the direction nor the magnitude of the resulting error is known. Selection for queries expected to benefit changes which effect is being summarized, while composition determines whether their untreated baseline outcomes are higher or lower than the remainder. The naive contrast can flatter the enhanced experience, understate it, or even reverse its sign; that uncertainty is precisely why it cannot be used as an effect estimate.

3. No overlap — the fatal one

Write e(x) for the probability that a query like x gets the enhanced path. Estimating τ(x) from data requires that both paths are actually observed for queries like x — that e(x) is strictly between 0 and 1. Under the current policy, e(x) = 0 for every query off the allowlist. Not small: zero. There are no observations of the enhanced path on those queries, and there never will be under the current policy.

This is categorically different from the first two problems. Composition and selection are biases — they distort an estimate we can in principle correct. Zero overlap is not a bias; it is missing data by construction. No statistical method — matching, adjustment, weighting, anything — recovers a counterfactual in a region where nothing was ever treated, because there is no information there to recover it from. The only remedy is to deliberately create variation where none exists.

The consequence

Our current setup can tell us how the enhanced experience performs on the narrow slice of queries we already believed in. It cannot tell us whether that slice is the right slice, how much value sits outside it, or where the boundary should be. The single most important question about routing is precisely the question our data is structurally unable to answer.

Part fourTwo axes, and how they interact

Given the definition above, there are exactly two ways to increase captured value.

Axis A — routing. Holding the enhanced experience fixed, move queries with positive incremental effect onto the enhanced path and queries with negative incremental effect off it.

Axis B — the experience. Holding routing fixed, raise the incremental effect itself: a better natural-language overview, better personalization of the result set, lower latency, better handling of the cases where the generative path currently fails.

They are not independent, and the dependency runs both ways.

Improving the experience moves the routing boundary. Every material improvement on axis B shifts some queries from negative to positive incremental effect. A routing policy learned before an experience change is partly stale after it. This means routing is not a one-time calibration exercise; it is a standing estimation problem against a surface that we ourselves keep moving. That has an important implication: the exploration needed to learn routing is not a project with an end date, it is infrastructure.

Routing exploration is what makes experience improvements measurable. Today, any change to the enhanced experience can only be evaluated on the allowlist — a narrow, favorably selected slice of traffic that is unrepresentative of where we would eventually want the experience to work. We are tuning the experience on the easy cases and learning nothing about the hard ones. Worse, we may be optimizing for the queries that were selected because the experience already handled them well.

Part fiveWhy exploration cannot wait

To be clear about what is not being argued: the two axes do not compete for the same people or the same slot, and there is no reason to pause experience work while routing gets sorted out. Both can move in parallel, and should. The prioritization claim is narrower — among everything we could start this quarter, the exploration infrastructure is the piece that cannot be deferred without cost to everything else:

  1. The expected routing gap is large. The current policy is heuristic and deliberately restrictive. A restrictive heuristic is very unlikely to sit near the efficient boundary, and both directions of error are probably present simultaneously — queries excluded that would benefit, and queries included that do not pay for themselves.
  2. Closing it requires no new capability. It reallocates capability we have already built and paid for. No model improvement is needed to capture the gain, which makes it unusually cheap relative to its size.
  3. It is the only improvement that can raise outcomes while reducing cost. Removing negative-effect queries from the expensive path saves money and improves the shopper experience. Every improvement on axis B costs something.
  4. It is upstream of honest measurement on the other axis. Until exploration exists, every improvement to the enhanced experience is evaluated only on the allowlist — a narrow, favorably selected slice. Axis B work done in parallel is fine; axis B work measured only on today's allowlist is running blind on the traffic that matters most.

The fourth reason is the strongest, and the one most likely to be overlooked when this is discussed as a straight prioritization question. Exploration is not a competitor to experience work; it is the instrument that tells us whether experience work is succeeding. That is why the sequencing question is really a lead-time question: the sooner the variation exists, the sooner everything else becomes measurable.

Part sixThe proposal: buy the information

Expand the allowlist to include a large uncertainty set — queries we cannot confidently classify as good or bad candidates — and give each of them a small probability of being routed to the enhanced path within the experiment. Several things follow.

Overlap is restored

Giving every query in the uncertainty set a small routing probability ε > 0 means the design now creates both treated and untreated observations across that set. For first-eligible exposures and query types with enough effective sample, τ(x) becomes something we estimate rather than assume. This is the whole point: it restores support and converts a structurally unanswerable question into a measurement problem, without pretending that every individual tail query will be powered or that later-query attribution is automatic.

The direct serving budget is bounded and set in advance

Aggregate exploration cost is roughly (traffic volume of the uncertainty set) × ε × (average incremental cost per enhanced query). Those inputs can be budgeted before launch, and ε is a dial we set. The calculation should use contextual unit cost and value where retailers or query types differ, and it should keep system-wide capacity and tail-latency effects as a separate scale check rather than assume low-volume measurements extrapolate linearly. Because the uncertainty set is large, a small ε can still yield substantial treated volume. At a fixed expected serving budget, broader low-probability coverage and narrower high-probability coverage are competing designs: breadth buys support, while depth buys precision. Which wins depends on traffic, representation quality, safety constraints, and how effects vary across the space.

Log the propensity at serve time

One implementation detail carries a disproportionate share of the value: record e(x) with every impression, whatever value it took after all gates and overrides. Logged propensities are what turn the experiment's traffic into a reusable off-policy evaluation dataset. With them, candidate policies whose actions stay inside the experiment's support can be scored against held-out logs, so one round of exploration can price many policies. The policy must be learned on different folds or time periods from the ones used to score it; uncertainty must be clustered at the visitor randomization unit; and weight tails and effective sample size have to be reported. Without the logged propensity, these evaluations cannot be reconstructed afterward.

The framing that matters

This is a purchase of information, not a gamble with the shopper experience. The planned direct serving cost is bounded and budgeted per learning cycle; scale-dependent latency and capacity effects are monitored separately. The return is a better routing policy applied to subsequent traffic. The asymmetry is the argument: we are currently paying an unbounded and unmeasured cost — routing wrongly, indefinitely, in both directions — to avoid a bounded and measured experiment.

Learn a policy over query representations, not query identities

One constraint deserves emphasis, because getting it wrong makes the project look infeasible. For tail queries we will never have the traffic to estimate a per-query effect, and per-query effects are not the goal anyway. The policy has to be learned over a representation of queries — embeddings together with interpretable attributes such as length, specificity, whether a brand is named, whether the query is question- or comparison-shaped, informational versus transactional intent, catalog density in the matched category, historical zero-result rate, and whatever query features are already available in our stack.

A contextual bandit or off-policy learner over that representation generalizes to queries never seen before, which is the entire value proposition. Stated plainly: we are not learning which queries to route, we are learning what kinds of queries to route. That reframing is what makes the tail tractable — and it also states the method's limit honestly: the policy can only distinguish queries its features distinguish, so a poor representation caps the value of even unlimited exploration.

The experiment, concretely

The design that fits our constraints is nested — two layers of randomization doing two different jobs:

  • Visitor layer (measurement). Visitors are split between control — status-quo routing, untouched — and explore. The graded A/B number is the session- or visitor-level north star compared by assigned arm: the primary policy intention-to-treat estimate. This layer prices the total cost of exploration directly and contains within-session spillovers inside a whole-visitor regime. Analysis and uncertainty still have to respect the visitor as the randomization cluster.
  • Query layer (learning). Inside the explore arm, routing is randomized per query: e(x) = ε on the uncertainty set, e(x) = 1 − δ on the allowlist, 0 on the judged-bad remainder. The primary query-level learning record is the first eligible query for each randomized visitor or session. Later-query records are retained for sensitivity and sequential analyses, but their occurrence can itself be treatment-affected.
  • The allowlist gets a holdback too. The small δ matters as much as ε. With e = 1 on the allowlist, the allowlist's own mistakes — queries routed that should not be — are permanently invisible, because there is no untreated traffic there to compare against. Learning to prune requires a holdback exactly as learning to expand requires exploration.
  • Every explore-arm impression logs the full decision. That includes the visitor arm, query order and features as available at serve time, policy and treatment versions, eligibility, e(x) after all rules, assigned and delivered routing, fallback reason, latency and serving cost, retailer, and the outcome window. That log is the asset the experiment exists to produce.
  • The learned policy acts only where exploration gave it data. Refit on a cadence; learn and score on separate folds or time periods; use clustered IPS and doubly-robust estimates with support, weight-tail, and effective-sample-size diagnostics before promotion to a live slice; and constrain the policy to the region the exploration actually covered. Outside that region — including the judged-bad set — it defers to the default. A model will happily produce confident τ̂ estimates in regions it has never seen; the support rule is what stops those estimates from routing traffic.
  • Exploration never fully turns off. A permanent small ε floor is what detects staleness after the experience changes — the alternative is discovering staleness in the quarterly numbers.

Use the randomized data to calibrate autoraters

Autoraters and randomized experiments are not competing approaches; they sit in a hierarchy, and the value comes from connecting them.

Randomized outcome dataAutoraters
CostExpensive, slow, needs live trafficCheap, instant, needs no traffic
CoverageSparse; hopeless in the tailDense; every query, offline
What it measuresActual incremental business outcomePerceived quality of the output
TrustworthinessCausally anchored by random assignment when the estimand and analysis unit match the designOnly as good as its out-of-sample calibration to randomized incremental lift

The right relationship is therefore: randomized experiments are the anchor; autoraters are the extrapolation. Use held-out randomized data to check whether treatment-control lift changes with the autorater score — calibration curves of incremental outcome by score band, not a raw correlation between score and outcome, which can merely rediscover that some query types convert more. Then use autoraters whose incremental calibration holds to extend quality judgments into the long tail where experiments will never have per-query power, and to catch regressions before they reach traffic.

The failure mode to guard against is adopting autoraters as ground truth without ever checking them against randomized lift. An autorater that rewards fluent, confident, well-structured overviews will optimize for fluent, confident, well-structured overviews — very efficiently, and regardless of whether that is what makes a shopper buy. Incremental calibration has to be estimated out of sample, reported by retailer and important query class where power permits, and re-checked after any material change to the experience, because a change on axis B can break a calibration that previously held.

Part sevenA dry run in a toy world

Every claim so far is a claim about logic, so it can be checked in a world where the truth is known. We built one: a simulated search product with 1.5M sessions a week, a ~4.6% baseline conversion rate, and a true per-query effect that ranges from −3.4 to +2.2 points of conversion and is positive for about 27% of traffic. The allowlist is the top 5% of a heuristic score that correlates with the true effect, with baseline conversion, and with noise — that is, it was built the way real allowlists are built. Serving cost is 0.2 points per enhanced query. Nothing about this world is calibrated to our product; the parameters are invented and only the shapes of the results carry over. Part eightThe batch version: learning the allowlist from the A/B we already run

Everything in Part Six assumed we build per-query randomization. There is a nearer-term version that needs none of it: the standard user-level A/B — treatment arm gets allowlist routing, control arm gets plain search — already gives a clean visitor-level policy contrast. It can also support query-level learning, with an important boundary. Randomization is preserved for a visitor's first eligible query, before this experiment has changed what that visitor does next. For later queries, treatment can change whether the query is issued at all, so conditioning on its occurrence can select different users in the two arms. One logging change is necessary but not sufficient: evaluate the allowlist in the control arm too and record the would-have-been-routed flag with its version. Use the first eligible query as the primary query-level dataset, report later/all-query contrasts as sensitivity analyses, and keep the visitor-level intention-to-treat result as the audited readout. Off-allowlist queries get identical experiences in both arms and remain dark — Part Three's boundary is unchanged; a model can propose where to explore next, but cannot identify off-list effects from these logs.

The obstacle is sparsity. Query traffic is Zipf-distributed: a few head queries carry real volume, and everything else is measured in dozens of sessions. In the toy world's version of this — 2,000 allowlisted queries, 150k allowlisted sessions a week, eight weeks — the median query accumulates 123 sessions total across both arms, and roughly 1 in 200 queries carries enough traffic to individually detect a +1pp effect. Per-query estimates at the tail are not noisy versions of the truth; they are essentially noise with a faint pulse of truth inside.

The estimator in one line

Fit a model f̂(φ(q)) predicting each query's effect from its features, then blend it with the query's own measured contrast Δ̂(q), weighting each by how much information it carries:

θ̂(q) = ωq Δ̂(q) + (1 − ωq) f̂(φ(q))ωq = the share of total precision contributed by the query's own data

Head queries are judged mostly on their own record; tail queries are judged mostly as members of their kind; everything in between blends in proportion. This is standard partial pooling (empirical Bayes / hierarchical modeling), and it avoids both failure modes at once: trusting noise, and ignoring data. One production detail matters more than the estimator's brand: give the query-level deviations heavy tails, so a query with real traffic that genuinely departs from its type can out-vote the prior instead of being shrunk into invisibility.

The displayed model is a useful binary-outcome approximation, not a universal likelihood. It is appropriate only when each randomized visitor or session contributes once to a clearly defined binary outcome. Revenue, counts, repeated sessions, and delayed outcomes require a matching model; all analyses need uncertainty clustered at the randomized visitor. In production, baseline and effect pooling should also allow retailer and time variation, because the same query can face a different catalog, traffic mix, treatment version, and value in different places or weeks.

Deciding what to remove — and what raw estimates do to you

Given posteriors, pruning is a decision under uncertainty, not a raw ranking. The toy rule below demotes a query when the posterior probability that its net effect is negative clears 0.8. In production, that threshold is only defensible if it follows from an agreed loss: decisions should account for posterior effect magnitude, query traffic and value, serving cost, the relative harm of a false removal versus a false keep, and any capacity constraint. The instinctive alternative — drop whatever has a negative point estimate, perhaps requiring statistical significance — is a machine for acting on noise. The bottom of a raw ranking is populated by the unlucky, not the bad, and a significance filter does not fix this: with sparse data, the estimates that clear a significance bar are disproportionately the extreme noise draws. This is the winner's curse, running in both directions at once.

The loop, and its one asymmetry

Iterated, this becomes a simple operating rhythm: run the A/B, fit the pooled model, revise the allowlist — demote using a traffic- and loss-aware posterior decision, add by supported model rank plus a random set — and let the next cycle's first-eligible data judge the revision. Additions are prospectively testable: a query added to the allowlist is routed for treatment users and not for control users, so the next cycle can produce the randomized contrast that tests it. Removals are not. A fully removed query gets identical treatment in both arms forever after, so the decision to remove it can never be revisited with data. The asymmetry has a cheap fix: demote to a probation tier that keeps a small share of routing rather than none — which is the allowlist-holdback principle from Part Six arriving by a different road. Two other practices keep the loop honest: make a small fraction of each cycle's additions random or near-random, so the next model is fitted on data less selected by the current model's opinions; and prefer expansion candidates near the support of what has been measured, since the model's confidence in query types it has never seen routed is confidence about its own extrapolation.

Structurally, this is a contextual-bandit-like loop run in batch: each allowlist revision is a policy update, the feature model is a proposed reward surface, and the visitor-level A/B remains the audited readout — it measures the value difference between successive policies directly, in exactly Part Two's terms. The ε-exploration design of Part Six is the same operating loop run continuously instead of in quarterly steps; the batch version trades learning speed for operational simplicity and inherits the same two invariants: keep a residual probability wherever future signal is wanted, and never let the model act far from its data. The practical punchline is conditional: if the current A/B has stable visitor assignment, a versioned shadow allowlist flag, a valid first-eligible-query cohort, and a metric that can be joined without duplicating outcomes, the first loop can start on existing logs. If any prerequisite fails, the visitor-level policy result may still be usable, but the per-query action list waits for corrected logging.

Part nineOpen design questions

These are genuinely open, and each needs a decision before the first experiment. They are listed here rather than resolved because the choice depends on constraints outside this document.

What the nested design leaves open

The two-layer design in Part Six settles the biggest structural question — visitor-level randomization for measurement, query-level for learning — but three things inside it still need decisions. Attribution: first-eligible-query analysis supplies a clean primary learning estimand, but whether and how to use later queries remains open; multi-search sessions need an explicit exposure and crediting rule if they are included. Granularity: whether the visitor layer is genuinely per-visitor or clustered coarser (per retailer, per market) for operational reasons, trading statistical power against consistency. The explore-arm share: how much of traffic enters the explore arm at all, which is a second budget dial alongside ε and must appear in the power and cost table.

The rest

  • What enters the uncertainty set, and how the boundary of "uncertain" is drawn — including whether obviously-bad candidates are excluded on judgment or allowed in at very low probability to test that judgment.
  • The exploration budget, and how it is governed over time rather than approved once.
  • Whether cost and latency enter as constraints or as prices. A single objective with an explicit price per unit of latency and per unit of compute is more tractable than a threshold, and it forces the tradeoff to be stated openly rather than buried in a cutoff nobody revisits.
  • What triggers re-estimation. The permanent ε floor supplies the data to detect staleness after an experience change; the open question is the statistic and threshold that trigger a refit or a rollback.
  • Which guardrails halt exploration automatically, and at what thresholds — specific guardrail metrics and thresholds.
  • How this interacts with retailer-level configuration, given that catalogs, traffic volumes, and tolerance for latency differ across customers — whether routing policy is global, per-retailer, or pooled with per-retailer adjustment.

Part tenRisks, and why they are bounded

RiskAssessment
Some shoppers see a worse experience True, and it is the price of the information. It is bounded by a small routing probability, confined to the uncertainty set, immediately reversible, and small next to the cost of routing wrongly for as long as we decline to measure.
Cost overrun Exploration spend is a dial set in advance with known unit costs and automatic guardrails. This is among the more predictable costs we carry.
Learning the wrong objective Optimizing to short-horizon engagement can degrade retention. Mitigated by defining the north star at session or visitor level and holding longer-horizon guardrails — not eliminated, and worth explicit attention.
Latency degradation at higher routed volume Serving cost and tail latency may not scale linearly, and shared capacity can slow control traffic as routed volume rises. The low-volume direct effect therefore needs a separate scale and interference check. capacity and tail-latency implications of the proposed exploration volume
Doing nothing Not a neutral option. The status quo is a routing policy — an untested, restrictive one — whose error we currently cannot measure in either direction. Declining to explore is a decision to keep that error unknown.

In closingWhat this changes

If the argument here is right, three things follow for how we work. Quality claims name their baseline, or they are treated as incomplete. Routing is understood as a standing estimation problem rather than a configuration file — a loop that can start in batch mode when the current A/B meets the identification prerequisites and graduate to continuous exploration — which means a permanent exploration budget rather than a one-off study. And autoraters are held to their out-of-sample calibration against randomized incremental lift rather than adopted on faith.

None of that requires a new model, a new metric platform, or a reorganization. It requires deciding that the most valuable thing we can buy right now is knowledge of what our own product does on the traffic we are not yet serving.
